PowerPoint · Scenario 1

Grounded content generation.

Turn a blank slate into a working draft with referenced content — sourced from a document you supply, not from the model's general knowledge.

What to expect: Artifact creation based on input file(s) — turn a blank slate into a working draft with referenced content.
FilesNorthstar Consumer Research Summary
  1. Put the summary in OneDrive or SharePoint. Copilot in PowerPoint references cloud-stored files. A copy on your desktop will not be found.
  2. Open a new, blank presentation. Then open the Copilot pane.
  3. Paste the prompt and reference the file. Type / to attach it by name, or name it in the prompt exactly as it is saved.
  4. Check the references, not just the slides. Open the summary alongside the deck and confirm the four findings and the open questions came from the document — and that nothing was added.

Grounded generation

Create a presentation from the Project Northstar Consumer Research Summary. Use the four headline findings as the slide outline, and format the messaging & the language at a Marketing Director audience. Add a slide at the end covering only the open questions listed in the document. Do not add questions that are not in it.
Notice what the prompt is doing. It sets the outline (the four headline findings), the audience (Marketing Director), and a hard boundary on the final slide (only the open questions in the document, and no others). That last clause is the interesting one — it is a constraint, not a request.

Reflection: The document lists exactly three open questions. Did your final slide contain three — or did a plausible fourth appear? Grounding is a claim you should verify, not assume.

PowerPoint · Scenario 3

Brand Kits — what, why, how, who.

A Brand Kit holds your organization's design system so Copilot can restyle an existing deck to brand, rather than you rebuilding it slide by slide.

What to expect: What it holds, why they matter, how they are created, who owns it — then a guided tour of an existing Brand Kit, applied live to an unpolished deck.
What

Your brand's typography, colour palette, logo variants, and imagery treatment, captured once as a reusable definition Copilot can apply.

Why

Styling stops depending on who happened to build the deck. The same brand decisions land on every slide, every time.

How

Built from assets you already own — an approved template, a brand style guide, an existing on-brand deck — then published for others to use.

Who

Owned by brand or marketing operations, not by each deck author. One owner, one source of truth, one place to update it.

FilesUse your unpolished deck from scenario 1Zava Corporate Template
  1. Open the deck from scenario 1. The generic one, before the template was involved — that contrast is what makes the restyle legible.
  2. Confirm which Brand Kit you have available. The lab demonstrates the Zava kit; in your own tenant, use the kit your brand team publishes.
  3. Run the prompt. Watch the restyle land across every slide rather than only the one you are looking at.
  4. Audit the result. Check the slides that were hardest to style — tables, charts, and anything with an image behind text.

Apply the Brand Kit

Modify and redesign this presentation by applying the Zava brand kit to this presentation. Restyle every slides in this deck to match the style guides, themes, colors, fonts, layout, logos, graphics, etc. from the brand kit.
Adapting this to your own work: replace “the Zava brand kit” with the name of the kit published in your tenant. If your organization has not created one yet, that is the takeaway from this scenario — the question to bring back is who owns our brand kit, not how do I style this deck.

Reflection: A Brand Kit changes who does brand enforcement. If restyling is one prompt, what is the reviewer for?

PowerPoint · Scenario 4

PowerPoint Skills.

Skills are reusable instructions you save once and run on demand — the standards you apply to every deck, encoded so they no longer depend on you remembering to apply them.

What to expect: What Skills are, how they are created, why they matter, where they live, plus tips and tricks — seen live against the artifact from scenario 2.
FilesUse your on-brand deck from scenario 2
This scenario is demonstrated live. The Prompt Guide contains no fixed prompt for it — and deliberately so. In the other seven scenarios you paste a prompt; here, the prompt is the artifact you build. Follow along and build one against your own standards.
What

Reusable, instruction-based capabilities that give Copilot domain expertise, workflows, and task-specific guidance — selected from the Copilot pane in PowerPoint.

Why

The check you do inconsistently becomes the check that runs every time. Consistency stops being a discipline problem.

Where

Custom Skills are authored as SKILL.md files stored in OneDrive, then toggled on or off in the Copilot pane — so the instruction is shareable, not trapped in one person's chat history.

Tips & tricks

  • Encode the check you repeat, not the one you did once. If you have never run it twice, it is a prompt, not a Skill.
  • Define what “done” looks like. A Skill with vague success criteria produces vague edits, and you will not notice until a customer does.
  • Keep the scope to a single job. Narrow Skills compose well; one Skill that tries to do everything fails in ways that are hard to diagnose.
  • Test it against a deck you know is wrong. If it does not catch the problem you planted, the instruction is not specific enough yet.
  • Name it for the outcome, so a colleague who did not write it knows when to reach for it.

Engagement moment: Name one review step you perform on every deck and never write down. That is your first Skill.

Outlook · Scenario 1

Inbox prioritization.

You define what matters. Copilot flags it as it arrives — and shows you the reasoning behind each flag.

What to expect: You define what to prioritize — Copilot flags what matters as it arrives and shows its reasoning.
FilesNone — this runs against your own mailbox
  1. Open the prioritization settings for Copilot in Outlook. Find where you state your own criteria.
  2. Enter your instructions. Use the example below as the shape, but replace the specifics with people and topics that are genuinely yours.
  3. Read the reasoning on a flagged message, not just the flag. The explanation is where you find out whether your criteria said what you meant.
  4. Correct the criteria, not the individual flags. If something was wrongly prioritized, the instruction is what needs editing.

Prioritization instructions

Treat anything from my manager or from [agency name] as high priority
Prioritize messages that include campaign launch dates or media buy approvals
Replace the bracket before you run this. Put in the agency, partner, or account team you actually work with. Prioritization has value precisely because it is yours — generic criteria produce generic flags, and a flag you do not trust is worse than no flag.

Reflection: Read the reasoning on one flagged message. Would you have accepted that explanation from a chief of staff? If not, the criteria need work — not the feature.

Outlook · Scenario 2

Govern your calendar.

State your working rules once, then ask for time instead of hunting for it.

What to expect: State your working rules once, then ask for time instead of hunting for it.
FilesNone — this runs against your own calendar
  1. Open your Copilot calendar preferences or instructions in Outlook.
  2. Add the standing rules below. Adjust the subject-line keywords to ones your organization actually uses.
  3. Then stop searching for time. Ask for it, using the second prompt.
  4. Check what the rules did after a day or two. Do that before adding more — standing rules act while you are not watching.

Calendar instructions

Automatically delete canceled meetings from my calendar.
Automatically accept meetings with "Customer Briefing" in the subject if my calendar is free.
Automatically follow meetings with "Office Hours" in the subject.
Automatically decline meetings that conflict with an existing event on my calendar.

Ask for time, don't hunt for it

Find me an hour of focus time every day next week, in the morning if possible
One of these four rules is stronger than the others. “Automatically decline meetings that conflict with an existing event” will decline on your behalf, without you seeing the invitation first — including the escalation that legitimately should have displaced what was already booked. Start with the rules you would comfortably defend to your team, and add that one once you have watched the others behave.

Reflection: Which of these four rules describes a decision you were already making by hand every week?

Outlook · Scenario 3

De-clutter the messy inbox.

Copilot-assisted analysis and guidance for organizing your inbox. Let AI automate the outcomes; you steer the goals.

What to expect: Copilot-assisted analysis and guidance for inbox organization — let AI automate the outcomes, we steer the goals.
FilesNone — this runs against your own mailbox
  1. Ask for a structure before you build one. Run the first prompt and read the proposal against how you actually work.
  2. Constrain the proposal. Note that the prompt states a preference — shallow, one sub-folder at most. Say what you want, or you will get someone else's filing system.
  3. Then automate one outcome. The second prompt creates a folder and a rule that routes into it.

Propose a folder structure

Look at my recent emails and suggest a folder structure that would organize it. I don't really like overly nested folder structures and would rather keep it at a folder or 1 sub-folder level max

Create a folder, then a rule that fills it

First, create a folder titled "Scheduled Prompt Runs". After the folder is created, create a rule that moves messages with "Your scheduled prompt is complete" in the Subject line into that newly created folder
The second prompt is sequenced on purpose. The folder must exist before a rule can target it, so the prompt says first and after explicitly rather than asking for both at once. It is a small thing that quietly determines whether the request succeeds.
This connects directly to scenario 4. “Your scheduled prompt is complete” is the subject line your morning triage brief will arrive under. You are building the filing before you build the thing being filed — so the assistant improves your inbox instead of adding to it.

Reflection: Copilot proposed the structure; you approved it. Which of those two is the part that actually needed a human?

Outlook · Scenario 4

Build your executive triage assistant.

A collaborative build — making Copilot work on your behalf, so a crisp update arrives on schedule, every morning.

What to expect: A collaborative exercise to show how we make Copilot work on our behalf — a crisp update delivered on schedule, each AM.
FilesNone — this reads across your own mail, chats, invitations, and tasks
  1. Run it manually first. Never schedule a prompt you have not read the output of. Run it once, now, and judge the result.
  2. Tune the priority list. Add the people, customers, and commitments specific to your role; remove the categories that produce noise for you.
  3. Then schedule it. Have it run each morning, so it is waiting before your first meeting rather than after it.
  4. Let scenario 3's rule file it. The completed run lands under “Your scheduled prompt is complete” and routes straight into the folder you already created.

Executive triage assistant — morning brief

Act as my executive morning triage assistant. Review my Outlook emails, Teams chats, meeting invitations, and Microsoft To Do/Planner tasks from yesterday afternoon through now.

Prioritize:
- Items received after business hours yesterday that may require my attention today.
- Messages, emails, meeting invites, or tasks that have not received a response from me.
- Requests, action items, approvals, customer commitments, blockers, escalations, and follow-ups assigned to me or implied as my responsibility.
- Any outstanding communications from my manager, leadership, customers, account teams, or frequent collaborators.
- Calendar events occurring today that require preparation, pre-reads, deliverables, or follow-up actions.
- Items marked important, urgent, time-sensitive, or with approaching deadlines.

End with a Top 5 Priorities for Today ranked by urgency and business impact.
Read what this prompt is actually doing. It sets a role, a defined source set, a time window, six ranked priority categories, and a fixed closing format. Every one of those is a decision you would otherwise make yourself at 7am, badly, before coffee. That is the whole argument for encoding it once.

Reflection: Compare the Top 5 to what you actually did that day. Where they differ, the interesting question is which list was right.
